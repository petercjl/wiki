# THE DISCLAIMER: COMEDY

## THE DISCLAIMER: COMEDY

Now let me contradict myself. A short film, particularly a funny one, can get away with breaking these rules. Humor can be an end in itself (though generally, it works best when it’s fraught with conflict as a character goes after what she wants, and that’s what makes it so funny).

But there is a danger with comedy or parody. The longer a short film lasts, the more the audience wants it to be a story. They want it to mean something. Sketch comedy, like what’s on Saturday Night Live or Mad-TV, can be very effective — in smaller sizes. Have you ever noticed when watching these shows that the longer a sketch goes on, the more it seems to run out of steam? Unless the comedy is downright hysterical, keeping us in stitches one moment to the next, our minds try to find the meaning of the events — how they will wrap up into a cohesive whole we can relate to. And with skits, this is rarely the case. When we can’t make the connections and find meaning even on a basic level, we tend to get frustrated and bored.

So the rule here is, if the script’s truly funny, from start to finish, with the biggest laugh at the end, your film may work regardless of many of the rules of drama. But if it lags in the least, if transitions reduce the effect of the humor and your story stretches out past five or six minutes with no real point, you may find your audience deserting you. (I’ve sat through films no longer than seven minutes checking my watch and wondering when they will ever end?) If this is the case with your script, try applying the basic principles of drama to your comedy and see if it doesn’t help you to develop a more cohesive plot line.
